How to spot fake news and fact-check a claim

A fake doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be fast, emotional and easy to share. Slowing down and checking the basics helps a lot. No method is perfect, but these steps work well.

Check the claim

  1. Find the original source. Who said it first, and where? A claim that only appears in copies, with no origin, deserves doubt.
  2. Check the date and the context. Old news is often shared as if it were new. A quote or a figure can also lose its context.
  3. Check the numbers. Look at the units, the scale and the source of the figure. Very round numbers, or records that seem too good or too bad, deserve a second look.
  4. Look for independent confirmation. See whether reliable sources, which don't just copy each other, report the same thing.

Reliable sources

  • Official bodies and the data they publish, with its date and method.
  • Encyclopedias that cite their references.
  • Fact-checkers that explain how they reached their verdict, not just the label.

Fact-checkers you can search for: Snopes, PolitiFact and Full Fact. Others include AFP Fact Check and Reuters Fact Check.

Read how a fact-checker reached its verdict. A label alone proves nothing.

Red flags

  • Urgency: "share it before they delete it".
  • Strong emotion, designed to make you angry or afraid.
  • "What they don't want you to know", or secret proof.
  • No source, or a source that is just "experts" with no names.
  • Screenshots or clips with no context and no date.
  • A claim that confirms exactly what you already thought.

Health, safety and emergencies

If a claim is about health, safety or an emergency, go to official sources first, and ask a professional. Don't act on a viral message alone.

The limits of this guide

These steps lower the risk of being fooled, but they don't guarantee the truth. Sources can be wrong, and facts change. The best habit is to check, and to stay open to correction.
